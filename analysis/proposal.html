Section 1

Retention stabilization & the Order-X threshold

Definition of "stabilization." The point on the lifetime-order → next-year-retention curve where the marginal retention gained from one more order stops being the dominant predictor of future behaviour — i.e. where the curve's slope has decayed to a small fraction of its early peak and further order-count carries little additional signal versus recency/behaviour.

Data-driven logic (from the completed retention study). Cohort frozen at 31 Dec 2024 (926,863 customers, 2.43M orders), 2025 = binary retained/not. We identify the inflection with four independent methods, not eyeballing:

  • Incremental Δ (pp per order): +9.2 (1→2), +8.1, +6.2, +5.9 (5), +4.9 (6), +5.1 (7), then +2.7 (8) and noisy 1–5pp after. Marginal gain halves by order 6–7.
  • Kneedle / max-curvature: objective elbow at order 7 (~48% retention).
  • Share of plateau reached: 80% of the reliable-range ceiling (~63%) by order 8, 90% by order 10.
  • Statistical reliability: buckets 1–8 each hold thousands of customers with Wilson 95% CIs ≤ ±2pp, so the steep early curve is real signal, not small-sample noise. Beyond ~13 orders buckets fall under 300 customers, CIs widen to ±5–10pp and the apparent continued rise is noisy — so the "flattening" up to ~12 is genuine; the tail is simply thin.
Conclusion — X is a range, ~5–7, with best point estimate 6–7. Stabilization is soft, not a hard ceiling. For the operational lifecycle boundary we adopt X = 6: Early lifecycle = 1–5 orders, Mature = 6+ orders. Rationale: by the 6th order the per-order retention lift has fallen to roughly half its peak and order-count stops being the primary discriminator — behaviour/recency take over. X = 6 also yields a larger, more stable clustering base (~20K mature customers as of Dec-2024 vs ~13K at 7+). The boundary is a single tunable parameter; 7 is equally defensible and the pipeline treats X as config.

Reliability: HIGH for the shape and the "diminishing-returns by ~6–7" claim (large n, tight CIs, 4 concordant methods, stable across the paid vs delivered definitions). MODERATE for a single exact integer — hence a range.

The cohort architecture: what is permanent vs dynamic

Six layers describe a customer. The critical design principle: identity layers change slowly and monotonically; state layers change fast. The ML model must never redefine a customer's fundamental identity because of one quiet week.

Why this split matters: CRM asks two different questions — "who is this person fundamentally?" (layers 1–2, stable, for strategy & suppression rules) and "what should I do about them this week?" (layers 3–6, fast, for campaign triggers). Collapsing them into one ever-changing ML label produces flapping segments and un-trustable journeys.

Early-lifecycle customers (1–5 orders) are not ML-clustered

They lack behavioural history and their order-count is itself the dominant signal. They get rule-based lifecycle nudges ("get to order 2", etc.) plus the dynamic layers 3–6. Only the ~20K mature (6+) population enters ML behavioural clustering. Keep order buckets 1,2,3,4,5 individual (retention shifts 6–9pp per step — high resolution matters for next-order triggers); consolidating them would discard the strongest early signal.

Section 4

Leakage prevention & time windows

The as-of contract

  • Observation date T₀ — the snapshot (e.g. last day of month). Everything is computed "as known at T₀".
  • Feature window — all orders with order_date ≤ T₀ (lifetime + trailing 30/60/90/180/365d slices).
  • Cohort assignment date = T₀.
  • Outcome window — a forward horizon (T₀, T₀+H], H = 90 days, used only to validate cohorts (did they repurchase?), never to build features.

Allowed vs leakage

  • OK anything dated ≤ T₀: RFM, category, discount, channel.
  • LEAK any order after T₀ used as a feature.
  • LEAK defining "dormant/reactivated" using post-T₀ activity.
  • LEAK using the outcome-window's category/GMV in affinity or value tier.
  • LEAK training clusters on all history, then "evaluating" retention on the same forward orders that shaped the features.

Architecture: Historical behaviour (≤ T₀) → cohort assignment (@ T₀) → future behaviour (> T₀) measured separately. For model development we snapshot at an earlier T₀ (e.g. 31 Dec 2024) so a genuine hold-out horizon exists (2025) to prove the clusters differ on future retention before we trust them.

Section 5

ML methodology (recommended, not yet run)

Recommendation: K-Means on transformed, de-correlated RFM+ features — with a rule-based RFM benchmark and density/hierarchical validation

The mature population is small (~20K), the features are continuous RFM-style (roughly elliptical, correlated), and — critically — the system must re-score new/updated users cheaply and reproducibly every month. K-Means gives stable, interpretable centroids and an O(1) "assign to nearest centroid" rule for scoring, which is exactly what a productionizable, versionable pipeline needs.

MethodVerdictWhy
K-Means (+PCA whitening)PrimaryFast, stable, interpretable centroids, trivial re-scoring, easy versioning. Fits elliptical RFM structure after transforms.
Rule-based RFM tiersBenchmark (ship both)Transparent, instantly CRM-usable; the bar ML must beat on distinctiveness & retention separation.
Gaussian Mixture (GMM)SecondarySoft probabilities → a confidence score per assignment; use to flag low-confidence users. More params, less stable at k selection.
Hierarchical (Ward)Exploration onlyDendrogram informs k and archetype structure; doesn't scale/re-score for production.
HDBSCAN / DBSCANDiagnostic onlyFinds natural density groups & outliers (whales/B2B), no k needed — but no clean assignment rule for new points and unstable in higher-dim RFM. Use to sanity-check K-Means and to carve outliers.
UMAP / PCAVisualization / preprocessingPCA to de-correlate before K-Means; UMAP only for 2-D validation plots, never as the clustering space in production.

Preprocessing decisions

  • Skew transforms: log1p on monetary, frequency, recency, tenure (all right-skewed).
  • Outliers: pre-carve extreme B2B/reseller/gifting accounts (a handful with 400–700 orders) into a rule-based "Wholesale/B2B" segment before clustering, and winsorize remaining features at the 1st/99th percentile — so a few whales don't dominate centroids.
  • Scaling: z-score (or RobustScaler) after transforms — K-Means is distance-based, unscaled features would let GMV dominate.
  • Correlated features: RFM components correlate (frequency↔GMV↔tenure); apply PCA whitening (retain ~90% variance) or select one representative per correlated group, so clustering isn't triple-counting the same axis.
  • Categoricals (primary category, discount class, channel): encode as numeric shares/ratios (e.g. category spend proportions, cod_rate, discount_rate) so they live in the same Euclidean space — avoids one-hot sparsity and Gower-distance complexity. Primary-category label kept as a descriptor, not a clustering input, to prevent category from hijacking behavioural structure.

Choosing k & validating

  • k range tested: 3–8. Selection by convergence of: elbow (inertia), silhouette, Davies–Bouldin, Calinski–Harabasz, and gap statistic — plus a hard requirement of business interpretability.
  • Expected final k = 4–6 (see §6).
  • Statistical distinctiveness: ANOVA/Kruskal on each feature across clusters; pairwise centroid separation.
  • Stability: bootstrap resampling + adjusted Rand index between runs; temporal stability (score at two T₀'s, measure churn between clusters).
  • Commercial meaningfulness: clusters must differ materially on the held-out forward retention/GMV — the ultimate test that they capture something CRM-relevant, not just geometry.

Section 10

Migration logic — existing users & new users

Lifecycle cohort — deterministic, immediate, monotonic

  • New user, order 1 → O1 immediately.
  • Each subsequent order advances the bucket (O2, O3…), in the nightly batch after the order.
  • On the order that takes lifetime count to 6 → enters Mature and becomes eligible for behavioural scoring.
  • Never moves backward (returns/cancellations that void an order are handled by the "paid" definition at compute time, not by demotion).

Behavioural cohort — stable, hysteresis-guarded

  • Re-scored monthly against the current model's centroids.
  • Hysteresis: only switch cohort if the new nearest-centroid wins for 2 consecutive monthly scorings and the margin exceeds a threshold — prevents flip-flopping.
  • A 120-day no-purchase VIP does not instantly become a different ML archetype — their activity state flips to Dormant (daily) while their core cohort holds until a sustained profile shift. This directly answers your example: activity state = dynamic; behavioural cohort = stable identity.

New / thin-data users — the eligibility gate

  • Minimum to enter behavioural clustering: 6+ lifetime orders (Mature) AND ≥ ~90 days tenure (enough history for trailing features to be meaningful).
  • Users who reach 6+ orders but have < ~90 days of history (fast-onboarding whales) get a transitional "New Mature / Insufficient History" state — treated with VIP-lite rules until they qualify, so a thin, unstable vector never forces a cluster.
  • Early-lifecycle users (1–5) are never clustered; they run on rule-based next-order journeys + dynamic layers.
  • Guardrail: any user whose feature vector is > N σ from all centroids (GMM low-confidence) is parked in "Unclassified — review" rather than force-fit.

Section 11

Model & cohort versioning

  • Model registry (dim_model_registry): every trained model gets a model_version (e.g. beh_v1), stored with algorithm, k, feature-set hash, training T₀, centroids and validation metrics.
  • Every assignment carries its model_version + cohort_version + assignment_date — so a label is always interpretable against the model that produced it.
  • V1 → V2: run in shadow/parallel for ≥1 cycle; build a V1↔V2 crosswalk (how each V1 cohort redistributes into V2) before cutover; backfill V2 on the last few snapshots to enable trend continuity.
  • Never overwrite history: old assignments keep their original version in fct_user_cohort_history; V2 is written as new rows. Analytics can always reconstruct "what cohort was this user in, under which model, on any date."
  • CRM handling of a model change: publish stable cohort names (not raw cluster numbers) and a mapping; MoEngage segments reference names, so a versioned re-map updates memberships without breaking journeys — with a communicated changeover date.

Attribute vs Event vs Segment vs Computed

  • User attributes — slow/medium state used to target & personalize (cohorts, tier, affinity, recency).
  • Events — moments that trigger journeys (order, cohort change, dormancy crossing, reorder-due).
  • Segments — built inside MoEngage by combining the above (see §13).
  • Computed/derived (keep in warehouse): the raw feature vector, PCA components, cluster distances, HHI, velocity ratios — analytical, not activation.
Do NOT push to MoEngage: raw ML features / PCA coords / cluster IDs, lifetime GMV to the rupee, every trailing window (L30/L60/L180/L365), SKU-level history. They bloat the profile, leak PII/analytics, and aren't used to execute a message. Push the decisions, not the mathematics.

Section 16

Measurement & the feedback loop

  • Per-cohort scorecard (monthly): forward retention, repeat rate, revenue, AOV, frequency, reactivation rate, CRM engagement (open/click/convert), discount cost, campaign ROI, and cohort-migration flows.
  • Does segmentation improve decisions? Prove it with holdout experiments: within a target cohort, randomly hold out a control that gets business-as-usual (or no touch); measure incremental retention/revenue vs the cohort-targeted treatment. Uplift, not raw response, is the metric.
  • A/B the framework itself: cohort-driven targeting vs a simple RFM-tier baseline — the ML cohorts must earn their complexity by beating the benchmark on incremental outcome.
  • Guardrails: discount cost per incremental order, margin, unsubscribe rate — so "retention" isn't bought unprofitably.
  • Drift monitors feed model re-training (§9): feature drift, cluster-size drift, silhouette decay.
